I am Eshita Rawle, a pre-biostatistics undergraduate student at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. My primary research interest lies in quantitative analysis pertaining to the cardiovascular system. Specifically, I aim to use mathematical simulation to analyze how atherosclerotic plaque buildup accelerates myocardial strain, particularly across high-risk populations that bear a disproportionate burden of cardiovascular disease.

My interest in quantitative analysis began in the 9th grade where I led one-on-one tutoring sessions, where I spent an hour on pre-work dedicated to preparing lesson notes for explanations of mathematical concepts (e.g. polynomial functions and rational functions), followed by an hour-long tutorial to prepare the student for quizzes and tests for Algebra II. While tutoring provided me exposure to apply quantitative reasoning, I wanted to see its application in real life. Consequently, during the summer of my junior year in high school, I began volunteering at a local community clinic in my hometown, where I collected patient vitals and inputted clinical data into electronic medical record charts. These responsibilities gave me firsthand operational exposure to daily clinic workflows. However, my conversations with patients outside the exam room revealed that patients avoided care due to costs, highlighting how socioeconomic disparities hindered healthcare access. This motivated me to explore broader population-level patterns of health care.

"I feel that it is very important, especially for us so singularly honored, to remember that science does not stand by itself, but is the creation of very human people." - Dr. John D. Watson.

Hence, through my AP Calculus final project, I used definite integrals to model vaccination rollout trajectories against infection rates across populations, examining public health through a quantitative lens. Calculating the area between incidence curves showed how small shifts in immunization coverage dramatically altered total cases prevented over time, demonstrating how quantitative analysis can guide data-informed solutions to improve public health.

I now attend UNC Chapel Hill, where I am completing core quantitative prerequisites for Biostatistics, as I prepare to apply to UNC Gillings School of Public Health as a Biostatistics major. While taking Calculus III, one of the prerequisites, I came across Dr. Boyce Griffith’s Cardiovascular Modeling and Simulation Laboratory online and reached out to learn how fluid-structure interaction models simulate cardiac function. Meeting with him and following his guidance led me to enroll in Linear Algebra in the Spring 2027 semester to build the mathematical foundation needed for computational research with broad public health implications. Pairing modeling with biostatistics will allow me to link physical insights to population-level patterns in health.

Alongside my coursework, I serve as the Social Justice Advocate (SJA) for Craige’s Community Government, where I design and execute community-building initiatives and advocacy platforms, such as Flock awareness, fostering an inclusive living environment. Concurrently, as a member of the Residence Hall Association (RHA) By-Laws Committee, I refine the existing policies to improve student experiences to eliminate ambiguities and defend complex legislative proposals before the Board of Governors. Together, these positions are an extension of my interest in public health, where I strive to contribute to the campus environment and well-being of the students.

As I continue at UNC, my immediate goal is to join a computational research laboratory at UNC to gain hands-on experience organizing, cleaning, and analyzing complex health-related datasets. In the long term, I intend to pursue a career in Biostatistics. Ultimately, I want to contribute positively to the healthcare community through statistical and computational methods to investigate health-related questions.
